01 / INTERACTION DESIGN

Neon Riff.
Making the first play understandable.

A solo rhythm-game prototype exploring how players choose a challenge, prepare for a song, and interpret feedback.

ContextUI/UX Design coursework
My roleSolo project creator
ToolsFigma · AI-assisted prototyping
EvaluationInstructor review

The problem I chose to explore

I created a game inspired by Guitar Hero for my UI/UX Design course. My design question was how to help a player move from choosing a song to attempting it without unnecessary confusion. The intended player is someone trying the prototype for the first time; this is a design assumption, not a persona derived from research.

I worked independently. My professor reviewed the project, but I did not conduct usability sessions with players. I can show the prototype and my interaction requirements; I cannot yet claim that the experience is easy to learn or that the changes improved performance.

One task, five moments

  1. Open Play Game
  2. Choose a song
  3. Select difficulty
  4. Prepare and play
  5. Read results / retry

The main menu is the entry point to play and secondary areas. Song selection groups track information with difficulty. Results provide feedback after the attempt and offer a route back into play. This walkthrough focuses on that core path; the presence of other menu buttons is not evidence that every feature has been tested.

Decisions and their limits

The requirements below were documented during the project. The alternatives are a retrospective comparison of design options, not evidence of a formal concept-testing exercise.

Give the player a preparation interval

Problem
Players need a moment to orient themselves before responding to notes.
Alternatives
Immediate start; a separate ready screen; a short countdown.
Choice
I requested a three-second countdown before notes appear.
Evidence / limits
The countdown was observed in the prototype walkthrough. Whether three seconds is sufficient has not been tested with players.

Make difficulty change the interaction

Problem
Responding to several lanes and tightly spaced notes may overwhelm a beginner.
Alternatives
Change speed alone; change active lanes; combine lane count, speed and spacing.
Choice
I specified fewer active lanes and slower notes for easier levels, and requested more space between notes.
Evidence / limits
These are documented requirements. Easy selection was checked; implementation across every difficulty and its effect on errors remain unverified.

Keep the visual identity, test its readability

Problem
The neon treatment establishes the game's identity, but glow and color can compete with legibility.
Alternatives
Keep the current treatment; reduce glow around text; pair lane colors with persistent labels.
Choice
The current prototype uses a neon palette. Reduced glow and stronger non-color cues are proposed next explorations.
Evidence / limits
The screenshots show the current treatment. I have not established accessibility conformance or compared these alternatives with users.

AI in my design workflow

INPUT / DOCUMENTED REQUIREMENTS

From intent to instructions

I used Figma's AI-assisted workflow to develop the prototype. I translated concerns about preparation and complexity into specific instructions: a countdown, difficulty-dependent lanes, and more separation between notes.

OUTPUT / REVIEWABLE PROTOTYPE

From generated UI to review

The result is an interactive artifact that can be inspected through the play flow. My role is to define and evaluate requirements; using generated implementation does not demonstrate that I manually programmed the game.

The supported before/after story is a change from written interaction requirements to a runnable prototype for review. I do not have an archived earlier screen for a visual version comparison here. I also did not record a manual baseline or time saved, so I cannot quantify an efficiency gain.

What I would validate next

I would ask first-time players to choose an easy song, explain the controls, complete an attempt and interpret the results. I would observe hesitation, missed actions and misunderstandings, then compare a clearer text treatment with the current glow-heavy version. This is a proposed study, not completed research.

The project highlights the difference between a functioning flow and a usable experience. The next step is to test the assumptions behind the flow, document changes with matched screenshots, and check keyboard use, contrast and motion preferences.
